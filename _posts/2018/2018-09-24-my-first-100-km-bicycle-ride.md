Before I start, let me mention, very clearly, that I'm just a beginner cyclist. I sometimes like to push myself to my limits to see where I stand. As I go through these experiences, I learn more about myself, and this post is just an observation, thinking it might help someone looking to go on a long bicycle ride; some things to consider, some facts, and probable best practices.

It had been long since I'd been on a long ride. The first long ride I'd been on was of 60&nbsp;km, on the Outer Ring Road, Bangalore. On that ride, I'd taken with myself a bottle full of ORS, and some packs of Appy. A few hours before the ride, I'd eaten a nice and heavy lunch. I left at about six in the evening and returned a little past nine. Overall, I'd enjoyed the ride. The gear I used for this ride was the 21-speed MTB, Hercules Ryders Contour.

I think it was in July 2017 that I went on another ride, to Chikkabhanavara. We started in the morning. By the time we returned in the afternoon, I was half-dead. Apart from the company, there was nothing I enjoyed on that ride---a stark contrast. I sat down to identify the mistakes I'd made:

1. I'd not carried food.
2. I'd not carried enough water.
3. I'd not slept the previous night.
4. It was too sunny.
5. I rode a bike that did not belong to me, so it was not adjusted to my body (Merida Big Nine).
6. I carried a full-sized, thoughtlessly-packed backpack.
7. My bum ached. A lot.

For the Sep&nbsp;2018 Century Challenge, I took help from Strava. I understood that I burnt roughly 300&nbsp;kcal in an hour of cycling. I drank about 600&nbsp;ml of water on a 45-minute ride. I knew I needed a sunblock or sun-protective clothing (but I did not care to wear either because the weather forecast said it was going to be overcast---I carried the sleeves and a sunscreen nevertheless). I was riding my own bicycle this time, and it had been adjusted to my body ratios to an extent. I carried two bottles; one on the bike, the other in a small backpack. In one bottle, I emptied two packs of Gatorade. I also bought four packs of Snickers---lots of glucose and some protein. I still had to carry a backpack, but I took only the essentials. Not even a kilo and a half. Not to forget, I wore padded cycling shorts this time.

Before I go into further details, let me mention that the ride was fantastic. By the end of the ride, I felt I'd accomplished something I never thought I would, surprised a few (which in itself is a dopamine rush), was assured of my capabilities and most of all, felt really confident, in spite of being exhausted and sunburnt, while my thighs craved for a massage. Here is what I enjoyed and learnt from the ride.

First, food is really important. Before starting, we were given a banana, some coffee and _chikki_, which is a brittle sweet made of broken groundnut and jaggery. We were also given a single serving of _Fast and Up_ energy gel "for the 70&nbsp;km mark". One blunder of mine was that I'd forgotten to pick up the four bars of Snickers that I'd planned to take along.

It started really smooth. Home to the starting point was about 8&nbsp;km, and we had to come back almost the same way. The first twenty kilometres did not even feel like a distance. We picked the Pipeline Road for the ride, in order to avoid the highway traffic. There were marathoners on the way, and that slowed us down a little in the mutual interest of avoiding injury.

I own a road bike: B'Twin Triban 100. It's an all-aluminium body, a single speed chainring, a 7-speed casette, the drop bar, 32&nbsp;tyres and caliper brakes. Things were actually fine almost until we got to the climbs near our destination, Thattekere. The climbs were really hard. This is when I realised the importance of the importance of my 21-speed bike. This 7-speed bike is good for short bursts of climbs of about 30&deg;, but not for long climbs of that gradient.

We still managed to reach the destination without much exertion. A co-rider (who had earlier said, 'So this is your first 100&nbsp;km ride, and your longest so far is 60? That's brave!', subtextually meaning, 'Dude, that's reckless!') asked me to eat well to refuel. I refilled my bottle as well, knowing very well that I'd need more than a litre and a half of water on the way back, especially, now that the sun was out (nope, it was not going to be overcast).

We started our return journey at about 08:45, after a fifteen-minute pit stop. The sun was still getting warm for the day. Also, the onward ride was down the Pipeline Road. This was really cool, given that you did not have to pedal too much, but this was some enjoyment which we were going to be charged heavily for, now, on our way back. By the time we crossed about ten kilometres from the destination, we could feel the sun on our skins. However, stopping to apply sunscreen and waiting under a tree for twenty minutes was not an option anymore, given that we did not want our muscles to cool down. Remember to use your sun protection no matter what you read as weather forecast.

We paid the real toll on the Pipeline Road. We were not disappointed; the Pipeline Road, combined with gravity, sapped every last bit of energy in me. I'd eaten a yoga bar, two bananas and a few grams of chikki, but this was not going to be enough. I have a high metabolism. I need to recharge more often than others. Nothing less than 300&nbsp;kcal of intake per 18&nbsp;km was acceptable. So I did another pit stop, ditching the others, to buy another banana. I couldn't eat more tha one at a time for the fear of bloating. I also refilled my water bottle (I'd finished 1.5&nbsp;litres in little swigs, in just 32&nbsp;km).

When I entered urban Bangalore from around Turahalli, a part of me wanted to stop once I was near home. The other part wanted me to go all the way to the BOTS store. I bought a bar of Snickers at a local store and ate it while riding. Bad idea. The bar was too sweet for me to finish in one go. It made me feel saturated and in general, a little sluggish. I kept my cool and waded through the Bangalore traffic that cannot see you if you're riding a bicycle, and reached the store. Lunch was served. I could not pedal back home; I was to tired for it, so, I took a cab home.

At home, I followed a few simple post-ride stretching exercises to ease the tension on my muscles. I took a shower to cool down. Even after the shower (and really, until midnight), i felt warm, as though I was running a fever. I knew this was normal after a strenuous workout---I've experienced it earlier---but not for that long. At about midnight, I had an episode of perspiration, after which, my skin felt it was at normal temperature.

The next morning when I woke up, I felt a minor pain in the thighs; nothing more. So, in the end, it all worked out very well. Had I been an experienced road biker like the rest, and not committed the couple of blunders I did, I may have enjoyed the ride even more. Here are some of the points that I think were lessons I've learned so far:

1. Never forget food when going on a ride outside the city.
2. Always wear the sunscreen or sun-protective clothing.
3. Get a saddle bag to store things. Get an opaque cover for the phone to avoid heating it by exposure to the sun.
4. Keep Snickers as the second option. Yoga bars, cake and fruit gels are better.
5. Always wear padded cycling shorts for rides longer than ten kilometres.
6. Check the maps to see what the slope gradient is going to be and choose the gear range accordingly.
7. Keep drinking water in swigs. Drinking too much at a time makes you feel bloated, and would make you visit the loo often. This isn't helpful in this scenario.
8. Try to continue pedalling on climbs, even if you're going slow. This keeps your muscles active. If your muscle cools down too much, getting it to work will be difficult, especially on climbs.
9. Try to avoid prolonged workouts on sunny days. Your body will not burn much calories when it's hot, in order to prevent heat strokes. This causes severe fatigue.
10. Do not ride in traffic if you're tired. It worsens your frustration.
11. Never forget to stretch after the workout.
12. Bend forwards when going down a slope; it makes you more aerodynamic, and gives you a higher momentum, which you can use on the subsequent climbs.
13. Try to avoid backpacks, no matter how small they are. On long rides, they can cause shoulder ache.
