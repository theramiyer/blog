Right when I was about to write on whether Hindi should be the national language of our country, something happened. We will get to it in a moment, but for the record, Hindi is not the national language of our country. No language is.

{{< toc >}}

## The misconception

I was under the impression that Hindi was the official language of the Union. Later, when I read about it, I understood that I was wrong. India has a long history. The most recent major shift was when the British Raj ended. At the time, English was the official language (for obvious reasons). The Constitution recognised Hindi as the official language of the Indian Union, but that was until 1963, when English got re-introduced, along with Hindi:

> Notwithstanding the expiration of the period of fifteen [years] from the commencement of the Constitution, the English language may, as from the appointed day, continue to be used, in addition to Hindi,---
>
> \- For all the official purpose of the Union for which it was being used immediately before that day; and  
> \- For the transaction of business in Parliament.

## The logic behind Hindi as the national language

India is diverse. We have hundreds of languages and dialects. In fact, our constitution recognises 22 languages as scheduled languages. Twenty-two. Experts broadly divide the languages spoken in India as Aryan and Dravidian. The validity of these is beyond the scope of this piece.

At the time of writing the Constitution, Hindi got picked as the official language of the Union, because Hindi had the most number of speakers (as in, people who spoke the language).

But given the way the States united to form the Nation, recognising it as the national language would have gone against the idea of a federal union, because each state had people who spoke their own set of languages.

## The idea of imposition

Urban Indians are generally known to be multilingual. Majority urban Indians speak at least two languages. But at the same time, one must understand that not everyone understands Hindi.

I feel that one of the major contributors to this idea is that Hind refers to India in languages such as Persian. And Hindi, in its literal sense, means 'of India'. Hindī Bhāṣā, in that sense, means, 'Language of India'.

The reality is that Hindi is the mother tongue of a little over 40% of the Indian population. The reality is also that those who speak languages like Magadhi, Avadhi, Bhojpuri, etc., report Hindi as their mother tongue (or consider their language to be a branch of Hindi). This is technically incorrect, as I have written in a [previous post]({{< ref "on-unification" >}}).

## The ground reality

I grew up in Gujarat. I spent over a decade in a remote town called Kodinar. What I realised then was that the majority of the folk there did not even understand Hindi. This was an eye-opener for me. I had thought, until then, that one could survive with ease in India if he knew English and Hindi. Yes, one can survive in most urban parts of India by knowing these two languages. But Hindi does not have the deep reach that one would think it does.

Of course, everything changes. The situation may be different today, over fifteen years later. But the point is, while Hindi has the most number of speakers, Hindi is not the mother tongue of the majority of India. And while urban India may be multilingual, the rural India is not. In other words, Bollywood and formal education are the two ways that the non-Hindi-speaking public gets introduced to Hindi. For those who do not have these privileges, Hindi is a distant language.

Most people get fooled looking at everyone online speaking Hindi. Do not be a victim of that idea. Most of those who have access to these resources have access to Bollywood and primary education. This is merely a part of the population. To the others, learning Hindi is a task.

Second, the "Dravidian" states. Tamilnadu has been at the forefront of the fight against what they call "Hindi imposition" (and rightly so, in my view). Learning three languages is impractical for most. Kerala manages to teach Hindi in schools. Their idea is that Hindi works in most parts of the country, and learning Hindi helps. From the practical standpoint, they are right as well. But again, in practice, not every Keralite knows Hindi. I have a colleague who studied Hindi in school, but cannot speak proper Hindi; he knows the bare basics. My mother grew up in Kerala. She knew slightly better Hindi when she got married and moved to Gujarat, than my colleague does.

Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Karnataka also teach Hindi in schools, but I have seen that Keralites speak better Hindi than them. Tamilnadu has been the slowest in adopting Hindi.

## The three-language principle

What often gets quoted in discussions involving "Hindi imposition" is the three-language principle. Central Government enterprises such as the Indian Railways follow this principle. You would see that stations have their names written in three languages:

1. English
2. Hindi
3. Local language

This sounds good. But the message that it delivers is that Hindi is a favoured language. Groups that oppose Hindi do not like this practice, because they think that it adds to the feeling of entitlement that promoters of the Hindi language have.

From the practical standpoint, this practice has its benefits. First, given that the majority of the population understands Hindi (given their exposure to the language), signage in Hindi helps a lot of people. Not everyone has access to English. English is a foreign language to anyone in India. Hindi, though, has close relationship with most people's mother tongue, and is easier to learn.

In fact, when I moved to Bangalore, I relied primarily on Hindi to converse with people here. Learning Kannada took me three to six months.

But the idea of having to learn "one more language" is a task. The point is, Hindi is not all that useful outside of India. Today, in a globalised world, we are better off learning English than Hindi, because in the professional environment, English is the primary language. Going by that logic, one could learn their mother tongue at home, and English for professional reasons. This should be enough.

Everyone has their own appetite for languages. I may have a larger-than-average one, but most are content with two languages. Not every Indian moves around all that much. Most do not leave their states. But those who do, have a smoother experience if they learnt Hindi.

## The magic of the local language

In the first eight years of my life, I had learnt a fair bit of Gujarati. I was still cute and little, and could get away with speaking broken Gujarati. When I moved to Kodinar, I realised I was in mini India---the company township had people from almost every state in the country. Hindi, being the most common language, was the language of choice there.

But the moment you stepped out of the township, you would feel the need to learn Gujarati. Given the environment I was in, I did not put much effort into learning Gujarati. But later I realised how easier transactions became once I knew the local language. I decided then, that at any point in my life, if I saw myself living in a place for over six months, I would learn the local language. And that means, if tomorrow I moved to Bengal, I will learn Bengali if I saw myself living there for months.

Language is merely a means of communication, no doubt. But we underestimate the gravity in that statement.

Language is a medium to connect. It goes beyond words. (Pun intended.) Knowing the local language gives you a glimpse of the culture, makes you a part of the society there. It gives a sense of sharing a common ground, more than the physical ground you share.

When I communicate, I try to ensure that the other person stays comfortable, even if I have to put a little more effort. The other person sees this attitude as openness and acceptance. The effect is subtle. I see the other person instantly open up a little more. Learning the local language is my way of being respectful---to the place, to the people and to the culture.

One can bully someone to speak the language of their choice and feel a sense of victory. But in reality, it does more harm than good.

Language is a means of communication, not competition.
