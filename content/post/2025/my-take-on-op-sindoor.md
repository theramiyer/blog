I distinctly remember what is now nearly four months old: I woke up for a drink of water in the middle of the intervening night between the 6th and the 7th of May 2025. My aunt was somehow awake. She said, as I woke up, 'We hit them.' The statement took a second to land, and in the groggy state, I just grunted. Aunt got confused, I clarified that it was an appreciative expression.

April 22 was a dark day for every Indian. Several of our fellow countrymen (yes, men) had died, with the women left to suffer the loss, and report to Mr Modi what they had witnessed. Apparently, the terrorists verified the religion of the potential victims before killing them (of course, they let the "true Muslims" live). It virtually boiled my blood. But isn't that exactly what the terrorists wanted?

What happened next is known to most of us. A timeline of what transpired through the weeks is [available here](https://csdronline.com/operation-sindoor-a-timeline/).

## The Resistance Front

_The Resistance Front_, or TRF, is rather interesting. The organization declared its existence in October 2019, about two months after the abrogation of [Article 370]({{< ref "kashmir-and-article-370" >}})[^trf]. The group has been known for "ubiquitous use of social media". After their first attack, they posted the following message:

[^trf]: The Resistance Front (TRF) ([South Asia Terrorism Portal](https://satp.org/terrorist-profile/india-jammukashmir/the-resistance-front-trf))

> Today's grenade attack is the inception of indigenous resistance of Kashmir to flush out the occupational Indian regime out of IOJK. The attack was carried out by our cadres, and in future such attacks will follow.
>
> ---The Resistance Front, 2019

The group claims itself to be a group of Kashmiri (not Pakistani) fighters, fighting for the independence of Kashmir. They call Indian administration the "Settler Colonial Occupation of Jammu and Kashmir", and their goal is to increase the cost of administration of the state, discouraging India from continuing to administer the region.

The Resistance Front (TRF) portrays itself as secular, yet its actions repeatedly reveal a religious agenda.

- October 2021, Jammu & Kashmir: TRF claimed a series of sectarian murders. It labelled Kashmiri Pandit Makhan Lal Bindroo an “RSS stooge,” justifying his killing, and later announced further attacks on anyone deemed “anti-Kashmir.”
- October 7 2021, Eidgah, Srinagar: TRF took responsibility for the murders of Sikh teacher Supinder Kaur and Hindu teacher Deepak Chand, accusing them of pressuring parents to salute the Indian flag.
- April 22 2025, Pahalgam: After attacking civilians, TRF militants interrogated victims about their faith and tested their knowledge of Islamic texts before killing them.

J&K Police Chief Dilbagh Singh described these killings as attempts to “create a communal situation” and stoke fear among minority communities, underscoring the overtly religious motive behind TRF's violence.

Also, what do you make of this statement:

> This is our land, and we will go to any extent to protect it. There's still time, leave our land, or we will be forced to step up action against you… The purpose of picking up arms is not victory or defeat. We want to show the world that you can cut off a Muslim's head but never force him to bow before tyrants. We will never become slaves to cruel rulers and non-believers.
>
> ---The Resistance Front

The links to Pakistan emerge for two reasons:

1. The Resistance Front is believed to be a front of the Lashkar-e-Taiba (or LeT).[^trf-let]
2. The Resistance Front has been reported to be linked to the Inter-Services Intelligence (or ISI) of Pakistan.[^trf-isi]

[^trf-let]: CrisisWatch 2025 -- July Trends and August Alerts ([Crisis Group](https://www.crisisgroup.org/sites/default/files/crisiswatch/crisiswatch-july-2025-asia.pdf))
[^trf-isi]: What is the Resistance Front? An expert explains the terror group that carried out the latest Kashmir attack ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/what-is-the-resistance-front-an-expert-explains-the-terror-group-that-carried-out-the-latest-kashmir-attack-250663))

Right after the Pahalgam Attack, TRF claimed responsibility for the attack. A few days later, they retracted the statement by issuing another statement denying involvement in the attack. They said their internal audits revealed that the credit claim was "the result of a coordinated cyber intrusion" ostensibly carrying "fingerprints of Indian cyber-intelligence operatives".[^pacnet35]

[^pacnet35]: The Pahalgam attack in Kashmir: Why we should expect a forceful response from India ([Pacific Forum](https://pacforum.org/publications/pacnet-35-the-pahalgam-attack-in-kashmir-why-we-should-expect-a-forceful-response-from-india/))

The article cited also notes how terror outfits make and deny claims based on public response. In case of the Pahalgam attack, the public response (even from within Kashmir) was sharply negative, with the people in Pahalgam and other parts of Kashmir coming out in protest of the attack.

In other words, playing the devil's advocate for a moment: 

Kashmir has, ever since the introduction of Article 370, along with Article 35A, had special status. Article 370 allowed J&K to have more governance in subjects such as education, ownership of property, healthcare, etc., have their own constitution and have their own flag (instead of the Indian Tricolour). Article 35A reserved the ownership of property in Kashmir for the locals, the jobs were reserved for the locals and so on. Since the abrogation of Article 370, nearly 85,000 resident permits were issued to non-locals. Also, nearly 40,000 Kashmiri Pundit families were apparently resettled in Kashmir.[^icct]

[^icct]: Operation Sindoor: a turning point for India in addressing terrorism in Kashmir? ([International Centre for Counter-Terrorism](https://icct.nl/publication/operation-sindoor-turning-point-india-addressing-terrorism-kashmir))

All this is likely to have angered certain sections of the Kashmiri community, making them feel their demography was being altered.

For the sake of the argument, imagine that some people in Kashmir do really believe TRF is a revolutionary outfit, fighting for their rights. Imagine that these people want Kashmir to be an independent State. The Resistance Front would want to be seen as a benevolent force by the people, which could later, potentially, go on to lead the State.

If the people saw the organization as operating against their core beliefs, and against the Kashmiri people (remember, of the 25 Indian nationals killed, one was a Kashmiri Muslim man), the people would no longer unwaveringly support the organization. It therefore makes complete sense for TRF to withdraw their statement and pin the blame on Indian cyber-intelligence operatives.

Is it possible that Indian cyber-intelligence operatives did carry out the cyber attack on TRF's social media channels? I think we are _capable_ of it. Is it likely they did it? I don't think so. Why? Because I see no motive in Indian agencies doing this. On the other hand, I see a clear motive in TRF (and by extension, LeT, as a proxy for Pakistan) doing this:[^trf-isi]

1. US Vice President J D Vance was in India, seeking closer ties with the US, as the trade war between the US and China was growing stronger. (China and Pakistan are all-weather friends.)
2. The Indian government had recently passed the Waqf Amendment Act, which, according to critics, diluted the rights of Indian Muslim communities. Kashmir, a Muslim-majority state, may seem to be affected the most by this. If TRF was resisting the non-Kashmiri Indians from going to Kashmir, building homes and businesses (since J&K was now a Union Territory), imagine how they would like non-Muslims becoming part of Waqf boards and tribunals.

Pakistan, for its part, completely denied involvement and condemned all acts of terror. What a surprise.

## The military response

After a couple of weeks of no serious action and exercises like drills keeping the Pakistani establishment on its toes, the Indian armed forces attacked terror establishments in Pakistan in the wee hours of May 7. The attacks were precise, decisive and proportionate. No Pakistani military establishments were hit, nor were there any civilian casualties. India made it clear that the action was merely to deter cross-border terrorism without escalating to a full-scale war.

Pakistan did not see it that way. They denied harbouring any terrorists, and therefore, to them, the attack was on the Pakistani soil. Coupled with the placing the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance being seen as an act of war, this "attack" on their soil was a bigger provocation. They launched an attack on our military establishments. We suffered some losses, but neutralized most of the attacks. Obviously, Pakistan also suffered losses.

What later became a prickly spot, Donald Trump, the President of the United States, claimed to have brokered a peace deal between India and Pakistan. The Indian government kept quiet until the 13th of May, and then denied Mr Trump's role in the ceasefire.[^trump-ceasefire]

Whether Mr Trump even attempted to broker peace or not, as we know today, Pakistan thanked him for his "role" and nominated him for the Nobel Peace Prize. That brought Pakistan closer to the United States, while Mr Modi's phone call to Mr Trump seems to have "inflamed the situation",[^trump-feels-cheated] which has also apparently led to the US imposing 50% tariffs on Indian products.

## The International Community

Some of this is a bit problematic. But it is what it is.

The United Nations condemned the Pahalgam attack, and pressed on "the need to hold perpetrators, organizers, financiers and sponsors of this reprehensible act of terrorism accountable and bring them to justice".[^icct]

During the conflict, the various governments around the world called for restraint, as usual, and offered mediation. India declined intervention, stating that all decisions taken on the Kashmir front will be bilaterally taken between India and Pakistan, and there was no scope for external intervention. This is in line with the _Shimla Agreement_. Of course, Pakistan did not think that way, because as far as Pakistan was concerned, they had suspended the Shimla Agreement.

Overall, to me, it seemed like a diplomatic loss to India:

1. The FATF did not add Pakistan back into the Grey List.[^bsgreylist]
2. The IMF approved the $1 bn disbursement to Pakistan as part of the $7 bn Extended Fund Facility bailout package, despite India lobbying against it, stating that Pakistan harboured these terrorists, and that the bailout funds could be used for terror funding.
3. Donald Trump, representing the stance of the US, wrote a post on X equating India and Pakistan.
4. Speculation of Pakistan's downing of an IAF Rafale jet using Chinese-supplied fighter aircraft[^rafale-speculation] was a blow that not only questioned the capabilities of Rafale as a fighter aircraft, but also portrayed the Chinese collaboration with Pakistan as a major setback for India.[^china-benefit]
5. The political powers in Pakistan seem to have "[unified] fractured political forces domestically", and the Pakistani military has emerged as more popular.[^atlanticcouncil]

### Lax Security

This was the summer. Vacation for children, more families out and about. This was also a pilgrimage season. This meant more unsuspecting people. If anything, the security had to be heightened. But that did not seem to be the case. The Baisaran Valley, where the terror attack happened on the civilians, was practically devoid of armed forces personnel.

Why?

Ms Priyanka Gandhi Vadra is right in stating that no self-praise by the government about Operation Sindoor could take away their culpability that tourists were left without any security.[^accountability-opp]

### Nabbing the culprits

What began as a terrorist attack initially claimed by The Resistance Front---and subsequently denied by the organization---ultimately proved to be a Lashkar‑e‑Taiba (LeT) operation.[^terrorists-three] Yet the narrative remains muddled. Eyewitness accounts speak of _four to six_ assailants, whereas the official NIA report cites _three_ perpetrators at Baisaran. Adding to the confusion, the J&K Police described _two_ of the suspects as _Pakistani_ and _one as Kashmiri_, while Mr Amit Shah, speaking in Parliament, asserted that _all three_ terrorists were from Pakistan---a position echoed by the Indian Army's official statement. The divergent messages make it difficult to discern a clear picture.

[^terrorists-three]: [Pahalgam attackers entered India from Pakistan three years ago: officials](https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/pahalgam-attackers-entered-india-from-pakistan-three-years-ago-officials/article69870527.ece) (The Hindu)

Can we truly fault those who question the credibility of either side's version of events in either instance?

On a related note, it is noteworthy that Mr Shah delivered the statement in Parliament rather than Mr Rajnath Singh. The J&K Police fall under Mr Shah's ministry, whereas the Indian Army reports to Mr Singh's ministry. Despite this, Mr Shah appears to align with the Army's narrative, even though the police and the Army issued differing accounts. He did acknowledge that _Operation Mahadev_ was a joint effort between the Indian Army and the J&K Police, yet it was the Army that neutralized the terrorists, and Mr Shah---not Mr Singh---spoke about it on the floor of Parliament. Our nation traditionally adheres to strict protocol; if parliamentary procedure was sidestepped here, it could set an undesirable precedent.

I make a conscious effort to steer clear of the narratives that mainstream television often pushes, since, in my experience, those broadcasts tend to tilt toward a glossy or bleak portrayal dictated by their advertisers, frequently diverging from the facts.

### The international response

First, in all this, the equivocal tone of "condemning" terror and not naming Pakistan was a big disappointment. And this, somehow, is either not being seen by people around me or the equivocal statement is being peddled as a "diplomatic win". No, it's not a diplomatic win. Everyone condemns terror attacks. That's what the entire world would do for anyone. Saying "The Pahalgam attack was unfortunate, and those involved should be brought to justice." is not the same as "Pakistan is sponsoring terror attacks on India, and is sending proxies to bleed India by a thousand cuts."

Had someone said the latter, that would be a diplomatic win for India. Had the FATF placed Pakistan back on the grey list, it would have been a diplomatic win for India. Had the IMF not disbursed the tranche of $1 bn, it would have been a diplomatic win for India. 

For now, we must live with the fact that the world did exactly what we did with the Russia--Ukraine conflict: We did not name Russia or Ukraine as the perpetrator. We just equivocally said that any unilateral aggression is bad. We did not vote in favour of or against Russia or Ukraine. In that sense, we equated Russia and Ukraine; the world did exactly that with us and Pakistan. So far, not a single country has acknowledged that Pakistan sponsored the terrorists to come to India and kill our fellow Indians.

### Opposition on ceasefire

While I cannot claim to know what the government was thinking, I sure can share a perspective. First, we had made it amply clear to ourselves and the world that Operation Sindoor, what happened on the 7th of May, was specifically targeting the terror infrastructure, which happened to be in Pakistan. We had made it amply clear that this was a non-escalatory measure. When the operation on the 7th of May was complete, the Indian armed forces categorically stated that the goals of the operation had been achieved, leaving the Pakistani military infrastructure fully unaffected, the civilians unharmed. This tells me that India never wanted a conflict.

Pakistan did not see it that way, and attacked the military infrastructure in India. India will never take this lying down, and so, the armed forces stood up in defence. We defended our territory, and to prevent more attacks coming our way, hit the military infrastructure within Pakistan, so that, they slow down and reduce their attacks on us, and eventually the conflict stops. Which is precisely what happened. We were merely defending ourselves. What would a prolonged battle with Pakistan have achieved? Territory? A surrender from Pakistan? And then what?

Wars are expensive. Wars bring losses. We citizens pay for these losses with our money. We lose fellow countrymen fighting for us. Families lose their family members. Wars are not like Rajnikanth movies, where one side comes back unscathed. In that sense, wars should be brought to a halt at the earliest available opportunity.

Besides, given how the world saw the operation and equated India and Pakistan, had India gone on the offensive with the upper hand we may have had, and brought Pakistan to its knees, we would have merely attracted sanctions on ourselves, while Pakistan raked in sympathy across the world, got itself "relief", and later, behaved like a wounded snake, only to send more proxies towards our homes. If the past 77 years have taught us anything, Pakistan isn't stopping any time soon.

### On revenge

I think this doublespeak about "attacking terror infrastructure" and "avenging the pain of our sisters and mothers" gives the wrong message. Our armed forces, being professional, were clear that they wanted to target the terror infrastructure. They attacked terror infrastructure. The attack was to prevent more attacks in the near future because these operations are like pest control at home. After all the spraying and gel dropping is done, we live in peace for six months to a year, during which time, the creepy-crawlies do not enter our home, as most of their colonies get killed. But some members of these colonies remain. They reproduce in those months, multiply, and attack again.

Terrorists are similar. As long as there is religious fanaticism, scope for misinterpretation, funding and incentive, terror outfits will keep cropping up. Will this ever end then? I don't know.

This operation did not kill the actual perpetrators of the Pahalgam attack. Sure, given the LeT connection, and that some LeT infrastructure was destroyed, some cells feeding TRF did get destroyed. But to be honest, if I looked at it from the non-Indian perspective, this does look technically "disproportionate". Also, hitting places deep inside a country's territory would certainly be seen by the international community as worrying.

Then came Operation Mahadev, after which, Mr Modi said that we avenged the pain suffered by our daughters.[^mahadev-varanasi] Well, justice is not about avenging a wrong. Also, as much as I love the _Mahadev_, I feel that adding a religious angle to military operations is inappropriate for a secular State (I mean "secular" in the "religion has nothing to do with administration" sense, not in today's populist political sense).

So let's save this eyewash.

### Intelligence failure

Here is where it gets complicated. On the one hand, not only was this an intel failure, but a major miscalculation on not having any security personnel in the Baisaran Valley area that is flocked by tourists. Tourists are easy hostages. Though, yes, typically the armed forces are the target, but when something as easy as tens or hundreds of unarmed and unprotected tourists are gathered in a place with no easy escape, any terrorists outfit would see it as a golden opportunity. We still have not got an answer to why there was such low security in the area. And this is a gaping hole. Those who question this are absolutely right in doing so. Some questions, yes, are provocative, but despite that, there has been no answer.

Secondly, when we say that there were 20--25 hardened militants are likely to have infiltrated in the past four years, are we tracking them?

That said, the other side of the story is that areas like the Pir Panjal range are treacherous territory. These are densely forested. And gathering intelligence in forests and finding terrorists who are constantly on the move is often a matter of coincidence. The forces have to rely on mistakes made by these militants, or for electronic clues. This is hard work with a low success rate.

### Military prowess

I have absolutely no doubt in this regard that we as a nation are doing really well on this front. The vision to Make in India or being Atmanirbhar was a good decision. Especially given the uncertainties in the world today, including the likes of the US siding with Pakistan, the situation that Israel and Russia (our strategic arms and ammunition partners) are in, the decision to focus internally on defence was a good move.

First, starting the Operation Sindoor itself. We knew the terrorists had come from Pakistan; Pakistan was not merely a usual suspect. What Pakistan did, stating that placing the IWT in abeyance was an act of war, we made it clear that any terror attacks launched from Pakistan, whether covertly or overtly, was an act of war. While we must be careful with this approach, we cannot deny that this time, not waiting for the international community to step in and instead, sending arms straight into Pakistan to attack terror infrastructure was a solid move.

Secondly, our striking Pakistan just a few hundred metres off Kirana hills was a clear sign of two things:

1. Our attacks are precise.
2. We will not tolerate nuclear blackmail.

Third, we proved our air defence systems to be remarkable. No system is a hundred percent perfect, but what we achieved was near-perfection. We neutralized nearly all the attacks launched by Pakistan. Using just Akashteer, built at home by BEL and DRDO. On the flip side, Pakistan, with so much equipment from China, could not detect our attacks on the terror infrastructure (which happens to be in their territory). As an essay put it, while talking about weapons for wars of today:

> In the skies over Pakistan, India didn't just dominate. It redefined regional deterrence. ... India didn't just talk about reform. It executed it. And won.[^swj]

> 
> India retaliated forcefully but stopped short of full war. The controlled escalation sent a clear deterrent signal: India will respond, and it controls the pace.
> 
> India handled this crisis without seeking international mediation. It enforced doctrine on sovereign terms, using sovereign means.
> 
> Critics who argue India should have gone further miss the point. Strategic success isn’t about the scale of destruction—it’s about achieving the desired political effect.

In an era defined by "forever wars" and cycles of violence without strategic direction, Sindoor stands apart. > 
> Operation Sindoor was a modern war—fought under the shadow of nuclear escalation, with global attention, and within a limited objective framework. And by every measure that matters, it was a strategic success—and a decisive Indian victory.[^spencerguard]

And even if, for the sake of argument, we consider that Operation Sindoor helped Mr Modi's image---so what? Nothing done as part of the military operation was against the will of the people of India. The entire nation---including me, often an avid critic of the administration---stood with the government and the armed forces as they went through this operation. I feel proud of how we handled it as a nation, when we could have easily slipped to the level of the shit-show some of our so-called newsrooms put out with a former Major making obscenely jingoistic statements about our strategic rival.

None of our politicians, who otherwise are among the worst lot of people in our country, behaved in a civilized manner, our office-bearers were clear about the communication, unlike the fiasco that their Pakistani counterparts created. Our armed forces, our Ministries of Defence as well as External Affairs and their spokespersons gave out clear communication keeping our fellow citizens informed and their spirits level. For this instance, I will set aside what the entire world says about the operation. I think we did right, and we did it well.

That said, we are a sovereign democratic republic, and I expect, as a citizen, that our government remains answerable, responsible and accountable to us the people of India.
