Last week, we traced how India inherited a colonial administrative machinery and ancient civilizational conditioning that undermined the Constitution’s promise of popular sovereignty. The Police Act of 1861 remains in force. The “steel cage” of the civil services still functions toward ministers, not toward citizens. When protesters marched in July 2026, the state responded with force rather than negotiation.

These aren’t anomalies. They’re symptoms of a deeper failure: the accountability mechanisms that were supposed to close the gap between constitutional design and operational reality have been systematically weakened. Let’s examine how and why.

## The Accountability Gap: Mechanisms That Don’t Work

One might argue that ministers and officials must hold real power during their tenure---that delegation is the functional mechanism of representative democracy, and without it, governance collapses into impossibility. This is true. One point four billion people cannot collectively sign legislation, adjudicate disputes, or administer districts. Delegation is necessary.

But delegation without accountability is not delegation. It is _abdication by the principal_ (in this case, the sovereign public).

The Constitution provides accountability mechanisms. On paper, it’s a robust architecture. In practice, most of these mechanisms are structurally weakened.

Between elections, the people have no direct veto. Once an MP is sworn in, they hold delegated power for five years regardless of shifts in public sentiment. If a minister misuses authority, proving it in court requires investigative agencies that are themselves often under political control. The system assumes that competitive politics and public vigilance will enforce discipline. In many constituencies and across many parties, neither assumption holds reliably.

The anti-defection law illustrates this perfectly. Enacted to prevent opportunistic horse-trading, it has instead become a tool to ensure party discipline overrides constituency representation. Legislators cannot vote according to conscience or constituent interest without risking disqualification. The whip binds them tighter than the ballot box ever could.

The RTI Act, once hailed as a revolutionary transparency tool, has been progressively eroded. Amendments in recent years weakened the Commission’s autonomy, stretched response timelines, and expanded exemptions for sensitive information. Citizens can still file requests---but getting meaningful answers has become harder, slower, and more legally fraught.

Judicial review exists as a constitutional safeguard, but the backlog makes it ineffective as timely accountability. Cases challenging executive overreach pile up in high courts and the Supreme Court. By the time judgment arrives, policy implementation is entrenched, political careers are secure, and deterrence evaporates. Justice delayed is justice denied. And in this context, it’s impunity secured.

Independent investigation agencies (the CBI, ED, and others) should function as neutral arbiters holding power to account. Instead, senior appointments are influenced by the executive, transfers are politically motivated, and investigations are selective. Those aligned with power face little scrutiny. Those opposed face aggressive probes. The agencies exist, but their impartiality is questionable.

The cumulative effect: between-elections accountability has become theoretical rather than practical. The public can punish at the polls every five years, but that’s a blunt instrument. It doesn’t correct course mid-term. It doesn’t stop abuse while it’s happening. It doesn’t restore trust in institutions damaged over time.

## The Party Trap: Manufactured Consent

If sovereignty resides in the people, then political parties---mediating between citizens and the state---ought to be vehicles for organising popular will. Instead, they have become ends in themselves.

Constitutionally, joining a political party does not diminish citizenship rights (Article 19(1)(c)). You remain a sovereign citizen with full rights. Organisationally, the reality is different. Once inside, you enter a hierarchical structure where strategic decisions---candidate selection, policy positioning, alliance formation---are made centrally, often behind closed doors, and communicated downward as directives.

The worker becomes a foot soldier: canvassing, mobilising, amplifying messaging, defending the party line publicly. The voluntary nature of participation creates an illusion of autonomy. But the choices available within the structure are pre-determined by those who designed the structure.

Consider candidate selection. In most major parties, local workers do not choose who represents them in elections. Party leadership decides: whoever is loyal, whoever is funded, whoever fits the strategy. The worker then campaigns for candidates they didn’t select, defends positions they didn’t authorise, and rallies behind alliances they had no say in designing.

The digital age has industrialised this extraction of autonomy. Algorithmic echo chambers narrow information environments. Micro-targeted propaganda, built on harvested voter data, delivers customised messaging that feels personal but is mass-engineered. Social media armies---formal “IT cells” maintained by nearly every major party---manufacture the appearance of organic popular support, normalising extreme positions and making dissent feel isolating.

Identity fusion binds party affiliation to religion, caste, or region, so that leaving the party feels like betraying one’s community rather than merely changing an organisational affiliation. Exit costs are engineered to be prohibitive. Quitting means burning years of networking, contacts, reputation, and possibly livelihood. Nobody forces you to stay. But the cost of departure makes it impractical.

This is not conspiracy. It’s organisational architecture. Parties are incentivised to prioritise survival over ideology, power over principle. Workers who challenge this logic are marginalised or expelled. Those who comply advance. The system selects for loyalty, not independence.

Two consequences emerge:

First, workers themselves experience diminished agency. They believe they’re exercising choice when they’re actually executing centrally-determined scripts. The satisfaction of participation masks the reality of subordination.

Second, workers amplify the hierarchy’s control over the broader electorate. A party worker shares a misleading post, attacks an opponent online, organises a rally for a candidate they haven’t vetted—in doing so, they don’t merely subordinate themselves. They amplify the hierarchy’s control over other citizens who vote, who read, who form opinions. They dilute other people’s sovereignty while believing they’re participating in democracy.

## The Electoral Myth: Voting as Meaningful Accountability

Voting remains the primary mechanism through which citizens exercise sovereignty. Five years of election cycles create windows of accountability. But three structural problems undermine this mechanism.

In a first-past-the-post system, a candidate winning with 35% of the vote claims a “mandate” while 65% voted for others. This isn’t accountability---it’s mathematical distortion. The system rewards polarisation, not consensus. It rewards mobilisation of loyal minorities, not engagement with undecided majorities.

In dominant-party constituencies, voters face a choice between the same incumbent and an uncredible challenger. The incumbent knows this. Accountability evaporates.

And across both scenarios, money determines viability. Campaigns cost crores. Independent candidates cannot afford publicity, logistics, security deposits. Candidates with criminal records backed by deep pockets defeat clean candidates with grassroots appeal. The system filters for wealth and ruthlessness, not competence or integrity.

The result: Elections remain necessary but insufficient. They’re the minimum requirement for accountability---not the ceiling. Between elections, citizens have no leverage. Within elections, the playing field tilts toward incumbents and financiers. The gap widens.

## Where this Leads

Accountability mechanisms exist on paper. In operation, they’re weakened, bypassed, or even weaponised. Voting remains the primary lever. But it’s blunt, infrequent, and structurally skewed. Between elections, citizens have no veto. Within elections, the playing field tilts.

The question is: if accountability structures have failed, why do we continue accepting them?
